2

Maximise absorption and minimise emission

The absorber's surface properties strongly affect useful heat gain.

High solar absorptance, \( \alpha \) More incident solar radiation is absorbed rather than reflected.
Low thermal emittance, \( \varepsilon \) Less infrared radiation is emitted from the hot absorber.
Selective coating Designed to absorb strongly at solar wavelengths while emitting weakly in the thermal infrared.
Just-in-time theory

A hot absorber loses energy partly through thermal radiation. For an idealised surface, radiative heat loss is related to temperature by the Stefan–Boltzmann relationship:

\[ \dot Q_{\mathrm{rad}} = \varepsilon \sigma A \left(T_s^4 - T_{\mathrm{sur}}^4\right) \]

Because of the \(T^4\) dependence, radiative losses can become important as collector temperature rises. A low-emittance selective coating helps reduce this loss.

3

Use the vacuum to suppress heat loss

The evacuated space changes the thermal-loss mechanisms.

Conduction ↓ Removing most gas molecules greatly reduces gas-phase conduction across the gap.
Convection ↓ Without a gas-filled gap, large-scale convection currents are strongly suppressed.
Radiation remains A vacuum does not stop electromagnetic radiation, so selective surfaces remain important.
Just-in-time theory

The evacuated annular space is primarily an insulation strategy. It reduces conductive and convective heat transfer from the hot absorber toward the cooler surroundings.

\[ \dot Q_{\mathrm{loss}} = \dot Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + \dot Q_{\mathrm{conv}} + \dot Q_{\mathrm{rad}} \]

The vacuum substantially reduces the first two terms, but radiative transfer can still occur across the evacuated gap.

4

Connect heat gain to thermal efficiency

Efficiency compares useful thermal output with incident solar power.

Just-in-time theory

Collector efficiency is the fraction of incident solar power that becomes useful heat delivered to the working fluid.

\[ \eta = \frac{\dot Q_u}{A_c G} \]

Here, \( \dot Q_u \) is useful heat gain, \(A_c\) is collector area, and \(G\) is incident solar irradiance. A simplified energy balance is:

\[ \dot Q_u = A_c \left[ F_R \left( (\tau\alpha)G - U_L(T_i-T_a) \right) \right] \]

This shows the engineering trade-off: improve optical absorption, reduce overall heat-loss coefficient \(U_L\), and manage operating temperature relative to ambient temperature.

Higher \( \tau\alpha \) More solar energy is available to become useful heat.
Lower \( U_L \) Less useful energy is lost to the surroundings.
Lower \(T_i-T_a\) A smaller temperature difference generally reduces thermal losses.

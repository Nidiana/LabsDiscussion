Measuring and understanding head loss through four network configurations

In this activity, you will measure and understand the head loss through 4 different network configurations. For each one: set up the network, take your measurements, and follow the guided analysis.

You will apply some theory previously learned:

  • 📐 Pipe friction — Darcy-Weisbach hf = f · LD · V22g = R · Q2  ·  compute Re, read f from Moody diagram
  • 🔀 Minor losses at T-junctions hm = K · Vin22g  where Vin is the inlet velocity at the T-junction (carries full Qpump)
  • ⚙️ Pump behaviour A pump does not deliver fixed flow. When network resistance changes, the operating point shifts — Qpump and Hpump change together along the pump curve.
  • 🔁 Hardy Cross method Config 2: guided step-by-step iterations. Configs 3 and 4: 2-loop solution computed automatically — you interpret the result.

Step 3 — Flow distribution in the parallel branches

You measured Qpump. The network has two identical branches in parallel. Answer the following:

A. What is the flow rate in the upper branch (LPM)?
LPM
The two branches connect the same two nodes (A and F) and have identical resistance Rbranch = 3R. For the head loss to be equal in both branches — which it must be, since the pressure at A and F is fixed — the flow must split equally. In a symmetric parallel network: Qupper = Qlower = Qpump / 2. This is the fundamental condition of parallel flow: equal resistance means equal flow.
B. What is the flow rate in the lower branch (LPM)?
LPM
The lower branch is identical to the upper branch. Both connect A to F with the same resistance. By the same argument — equal resistance, fixed pressure difference — both branches carry the same flow: Qlower = Qpump / 2. The flow splits equally, not because of a rule, but because symmetry forces it.
C. What is the head loss across the three pipes in series in the upper branch (mmH₂O)?
mmH₂O
In series the head losses add: Hbranch = hpipe1 + hpipe2 + hpipe3 = 3 × hpipe2. Each pipe carries the same flow and has the same resistance, so each contributes equally.
D. What is the head loss across the lower branch (mmH₂O)?
mmH₂O
In pipes in parallel, both branches connect the same two nodes A and F. The head loss across each branch must be equal — it is the same pressure difference, whichever path you take. Hlower = Hupper = 3 × hpipe2.

Question 1 — real engineering context
"Your pipes have L/D = 60. A typical water supply main is 500 m long with diameter 200 mm (L/D = 2 500). Estimate what fraction of the total head loss minor losses would represent in that main. Compare with your experiment — what changes and why?"
Explanation Minor losses scale as K · V2/2g — a fixed energy cost per junction, independent of pipe length. Pipe friction scales as f · (L/D) · V2/2g — it grows with L/D. In your experiment L/D = 60, so pipe friction is small and minor losses dominate (~75% of the total). In a water supply main with L/D = 2 500, the pipe friction term is ~42× larger relative to minor losses. Minor losses would represent roughly 2–5% of the total head loss — negligible in practice. The ratio of minor to pipe friction losses scales as 1/(f · L/D). As L/D increases, minor losses become progressively less important.
Question 2 — design priorities
"In your experiment minor losses dominate. In a real city water network pipe friction dominates. As a civil engineer designing each type of system, which losses must you prioritise — and what does this mean for how you model the network?"
Explanation In compact systems — HVAC, laboratory networks, building services — minor losses are significant and must be modelled explicitly. Every fitting, valve and junction has a K value and contributes meaningfully to the total head loss. Neglecting them leads to undersized pumps and insufficient pressure at delivery points. In city water distribution networks, pipes are hundreds of metres long and minor losses are typically less than 5% of the total. Engineers use simplified Hardy Cross models with pipe friction only, and the results are accurate enough for design purposes. The key skill is knowing which regime you are in — and your experiment makes that distinction physically tangible.

Discuss with your group
"Hardy Cross assumes f is unchanged throughout the iterations — it is calculated once and held fixed. This is a very good assumption in real water mains where flow is fully turbulent and f depends only on pipe roughness. In your experiment the flow is in the transition zone. How does this affect the result? What does the comparison between your Hardy Cross estimate and your experimental Hfriction tell you?"
Hint Hardy Cross assumes f is constant for each pipe throughout the iterations — it does not re-compute f as Q changes. In fully turbulent flow (real water mains, rough pipes, high Re) f genuinely is constant — it depends only on ε/D and is independent of Re. So the assumption is exact. In your experiment, the pipes are smooth and flow is in the transition zone where f depends strongly on Re. Each pipe in Config 3 carries a different Q, so each has a slightly different Re and therefore a slightly different f. The f used in the Hardy Cross calculation was measured in Config 1 at a different flow rate — this is the source of any discrepancy you observe.
